Second Unit Notes

On the parts of a generative video prompt that are direction, not specification.

Fifteen seconds is enough for three acts, and that is why most short prompts fail

The hardest thing to get out of a short generative clip is not detail, it is structure. A prompt that describes a situation produces a moving photograph; a prompt that describes a change produces something that reads as a film.

Write a prompt that describes a scene and you get a moving photograph: a person in a place, doing something, indefinitely. Everything in frame is correct and nothing happens. This is the single most common disappointment with generated video, and it is almost always a structural problem rather than a quality one.

The fix is not more description. It is a change of state, written explicitly.

The oldest version, and still the most reliable at this length:

Act 1   A problem, embodied in something visible.
Act 2   An intervention.
Act 3   The same situation, visibly changed.

Too hot. Buys fan. Feels better. Three beats, fifteen seconds, and the result reads as a piece of film rather than a clip, because the last shot means something different from the first.

Why the model will not supply this for you

A generative video model is a very good scene renderer and an indifferent dramatist. Give it a situation and it will render the situation. Nothing in the objective it was trained against rewards a state change across the clip — coherence, yes; consequence, no.

So structure is a thing you have to specify, and the specification is short:

  • Name the before-state as something visible. Not "she is hot" — a damp hairline, a hand fanning a face, light shimmering off asphalt. The same rule as everywhere else: if a camera cannot see it, the model cannot render it.
  • Name the intervention as a physical action. The object enters frame, is picked up, is switched on. An intervention that happens off-screen or by implication does not happen.
  • Name the after-state as the same visible thing, changed. Hair lifting in the airflow, the fanning hand dropping, shoulders coming down.

The third bullet is the one people skip, and skipping it is what turns three acts back into two. The after-state has to rhyme with the before-state — same body part, same detail, visibly different. That rhyme is what makes the change readable in four seconds.

Budgeting the acts

At fifteen seconds the temptation is to split evenly. Don't. Roughly:

ActShareWhy
Problem~30%Needs to be established but is the least interesting part
Intervention~25%Shortest — it is a single action
Result~45%Where the payoff lives, and where the product is on screen

If the piece has dialogue, one line per act is the natural allocation, and it is worth writing them as one line per act rather than as a continuous monologue — a line break is where a cut can go.

At eight seconds rather than fifteen, drop to two acts. Three acts in eight seconds gives each about two and a half seconds, which is under the floor for a state change to be legible. Better to do two beats well than three badly.

The structure is not the only one, just the default

Two other structures work at this length, and both have the same property of being a change rather than a situation:

Reveal. The frame shows something ambiguous; the camera or the subject moves; the thing turns out to be something else. One state change, positioned at the end.

Escalation. The same action repeats with increasing intensity. Structurally this is the weakest of the three, because it has no turn — but it is the easiest to specify and it suits montage.

What does not work at this length is anything requiring the viewer to hold two threads. There is no room to cut away and come back.

The test

Read your prompt and ask: is the last shot different in meaning from the first? Not different in content — different in meaning. If a viewer could watch the clip backwards and not notice, there is no structure in it, and no amount of additional description will add any.

A worked version of the three-act form, with the timings and one line of dialogue per act, is the oldest advertising structure there is, written out across fifteen seconds. Prompts built on the reveal and escalation structures instead are in the same library at minimax-h3ai.video.
