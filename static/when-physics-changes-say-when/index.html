Second Unit Notes

On the parts of a generative video prompt that are direction, not specification.

If the physics changes mid-clip, the prompt has to say when

A rule the whole clip obeys is cheap to specify. A rule that changes three times is not, because the model has no way to know where the boundaries are unless you put them on a clock.

Most prompts establish a physical world once and leave it alone. Gravity points down, objects fall, people stand on floors. You do not write any of that, because it is the default.

The interesting case is a world whose rules change during the shot — gravity that flickers off, direction that reverses, a rotating environment where "down" depends on where you are standing. Written casually, this produces mush: the model averages the states and you get a clip where everything drifts vaguely and nothing reads as a change.

The fix is the same one that works for shot scheduling, applied to physics: put the state changes on a clock.

0.0–3.0s   weightless; camera tumbles; subject pushes off walls
3.0–6.0s   gravity flickers violently
6.0–9.0s   full zero-g; bodies and debris spin freely
9.0–12.0s  gravity repeatedly shifts direction
12.0–15.0s pull back; the rotating structure is revealed

Five blocks, three seconds each. Each block names one physical regime. The model is no longer asked "what does inconsistent gravity look like" — it is asked five separate, answerable questions.

Why even spacing matters more here than elsewhere

In an ordinary timed shot list, uneven blocks are good: a held beat wants four seconds, a whip-pan wants half of one. For a physics sequence, regular blocks are usually better, and it is worth understanding why.

A physics change is only legible if the viewer sees the state before and the state after. That needs enough time on each side to establish a baseline. Three seconds is roughly the floor — under about two, a regime change reads as a glitch rather than an event, because there was never a stable state to depart from.

So the constraint is not aesthetic. Each regime needs enough duration to become the expectation that the next one violates.

The last block is doing something different

Look at the structure again. The first four blocks are inside the chaos: close, confusing, disorienting on purpose. The fifth pulls back and reveals the whole rotating structure from outside.

That final wide shot is what makes the first twelve seconds legible in retrospect. Without it the clip is just confusion; with it, the confusion was setup. This is an ordinary piece of film grammar — the establishing shot moved to the end — and it is worth writing deliberately, because a model given five equally chaotic blocks will give you five equally chaotic blocks.

The general form: if the middle of your clip is intentionally disorienting, one block has to be the one that explains it, and you should decide whether it goes first or last. Last is usually stronger, and it is what a model will never choose for you.

The supporting cast exists to make the rule visible

The prompt has soldiers, a fight, an exploding bulkhead, drifting debris. None of that is the subject. Physics is invisible without objects to act on it — you cannot see gravity change direction in an empty corridor.

Which suggests a practical test: for each block, name at least one object whose behaviour differs between this regime and the previous one. Debris that fell in block one and floats in block three. A body that pushed off a wall and now tumbles. If a block does not have such an object, the regime change in it will not be visible, and the block is doing nothing.

Three failure modes worth naming in the prompt

  • Averaging. Without a clock, the model blends the regimes into a single mushy "kind of floaty" state. The timestamps are the whole fix.
  • Cutting instead of changing. A gravity change is not a cut. If you want continuity through the change, say the camera does not cut — otherwise a block boundary is an invitation to one.
  • Reset drift. Each block is a fresh description, so the environment can quietly change with it: different corridor, different lighting, different number of people. Restate what persists — the location, the character, the light source — in the blocks where it matters.

The worked example is a fifteen-second sequence where gravity changes direction on a three-second clock, including the pull-back that pays off the first four blocks. Other prompts using timed blocks for things other than shot changes are indexed at minimax-h3ai.video.
