Second Unit Notes

On the parts of a generative video prompt that are direction, not specification.

The smallest honest test of whether a video model can talk is one static shot

Everyone benchmarks lip sync inside a montage, where a cut every second hides the failure. The test that actually tells you something spends its entire budget on a single locked-off frame and one spoken line.

If you want to know whether a video model can generate speech that matches a face, the instinct is to write something impressive: a few locations, some camera movement, a couple of speakers. That test tells you almost nothing, because cutting is the oldest lip-sync fix in cinema. Every cut is a place where the mouth resets, and a montage with a cut per second never holds a mouth on screen long enough for the sync to visibly drift.

The test that tells you something is deliberately unimpressive:

  • One shot. No cuts.
  • Locked-off camera. No push, no orbit, no handheld.
  • One speaker, facing camera. No profile, no over-the-shoulder.
  • One line, long enough to drift. Twenty-odd words is about right — long enough that a phase error accumulates, short enough to fit a clip.
  • Nothing else. No scene change, no props doing anything, no second subject.

That is roughly three hundred characters of prompt against a cap in the thousands, and it is the correct length, because every word you add is budget taken away from the only thing being measured.

What you are actually looking at

Watch for four failures, in this order. They fail in this order too.

1. Phase. Does the mouth move at the same time as the audio, or a beat behind? Easiest to see on plosives — the lips should close before the b or p sounds, not on them.

2. Viseme shape. Are the mouth shapes right for the sounds, or is it a generic chewing motion timed to the amplitude envelope? An oo and an ee look nothing alike. Amplitude-driven animation makes them identical.

3. Drift. Sync at second one means little. Sync at second seven is the result. A model that resets phase at every cut will drift visibly across an unbroken eight seconds, which is exactly why the montage version hides it.

4. Idle behaviour. What does the face do between words, and what does the body do while the face talks? A head that is perfectly still is as wrong as one that bobs on every syllable. Blinks, small weight shifts, a breath before a long clause — these are what separate "a talking face" from "a person talking".

Why "no camera move" is doing real work

A moving camera is a mercy. Parallax, motion blur and changing angle all reduce how much of the mouth you can scrutinise in any single frame, and a slow push-in gives the eye something else to track. Lock the camera off and there is nothing to look at but the failure.

Same reason for a front-on angle rather than a three-quarter one. Three-quarter hides the far corner of the mouth, which is where asymmetry shows up first.

So the sparseness is not minimalism for its own sake. Each removed element is a removed alibi.

Two things to write into the prompt that people leave out

Say what the delivery is, not just what the words are. "Speaking softly, low tones, whispering" is an instruction about the audio, and it constrains the video too, because whispered speech has a smaller mouth aperture and less jaw movement. Give the model only the words and you get a default declamatory reading that matches nothing.

Say no subtitles. Models trained on a lot of captioned social video will cheerfully burn text into the frame. If your test is about the mouth, an on-screen caption is both a distraction and a second failure mode you did not ask to evaluate.

When to graduate from this test

Once a model passes the single-shot version, the interesting failures move somewhere else entirely: identity drift across cuts, two speakers competing in the mix, dialogue that has to land at a specific timestamp. Those are worth testing, and they are different tests. Running them first, before the one-shot baseline passes, means every failure has three possible causes and you cannot tell them apart.

Start with the boring one. It has one variable.

The version I use as a baseline is a twenty-two word talking-head prompt with nothing in it but the performance — one static shot, one line, no cuts. The prompts that break each of the four failures above, and the model's published dialogue-language support, are at https://minimax-h3ai.video.
