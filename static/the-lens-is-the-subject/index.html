Second Unit Notes

On the parts of a generative video prompt that are direction, not specification.

Sometimes the lens is the subject and the clothes are set dressing

A prompt can name a genre, a wardrobe and six locations and still be, structurally, an instruction about one piece of glass. Recognising which element is load-bearing changes what you spend words on.

Here is a prompt described by its contents: thirteen seconds, six locations, a streetwear wardrobe, a specific city, and a fisheye lens.

Here is the same prompt described by what is actually doing the work: a fisheye lens, applied to six things.

Those are different prompts to write, and the difference decides where the words go. If the clothes are the subject, you spend your budget on garment detail and hold each look long enough to read. If the lens is the subject, you spend it on what the lens does — barrel distortion, the way straight architecture bends at frame edges, how a subject's hand reads when it comes toward the glass — and the six locations exist to give that distortion six different things to bend.

How to tell which element is load-bearing

Ask: if I removed this element, would the piece still be recognisably itself?

Take the fisheye out and put the same six locations and the same wardrobe on a normal lens, and you have generic fashion b-roll. Take the wardrobe out and shoot six locations on a fisheye and you still have the same video with different clothes. The lens survives the swap; the clothes do not. The lens is the subject.

Run that test on your own prompt before you write it out in full. It usually reveals that one element is carrying the piece and three or four others are interchangeable — and interchangeable elements do not need much description, because the model will produce something reasonable and nothing depends on which reasonable thing it picks.

What a lens instruction has to include

"Fisheye" alone is a weak instruction, in the same way "dolly in" is a weak camera instruction. What makes it work:

  • The distortion, described as behaviour. Straight lines bowing outward, verticals curving at the edges, the centre magnified relative to the periphery.
  • Proximity. Ultra-wide glass is only interesting close. A fisheye at three metres is just a wide shot with soft corners; at thirty centimetres it is the whole point.
  • What comes toward the lens. A hand, a shoe, a zip pull — ultra-wide lenses exaggerate anything approaching the front element, and that exaggeration is the effect people actually want when they ask for a fisheye.
  • What the architecture does. The reason to shoot six locations rather than one is that different geometry bends differently. A straight corridor, a low ceiling and an open intersection are three separate demonstrations.

Notice that all four are describing consequences, not naming the equipment. The model has no lens. It has a distribution of images, and what you are steering toward is the visual signature, which is best specified by its effects.

The corollary: name the first cut

A related habit worth stealing from that prompt. Its opening beat is a zip pull moving toward the lens — a small, close, high-distortion action that establishes the visual language immediately.

The first beat of a stylistically unusual piece should be the beat that teaches the viewer the style. If your distinctive element does not appear until second four, the first three seconds read as ordinary and the change reads as inconsistency. Open on the thing that is weird.

Where this generalises

The same analysis applies whenever a piece has one dominant formal choice:

Load-bearing elementWhat the other elements become
A lens or focal lengthLocations exist to be distorted differently
A single unbroken takeBlocking exists to justify the camera path
A colour restrictionWardrobe and set exist to stay inside the palette
A frozen subjectEverything else exists to prove depth
A rhythm or cut rateContent exists to fill the slots

In every row, the mistake is the same: spending the prompt on the right-hand column. The right-hand column is where the model's defaults are good enough. The left-hand column is where it needs you.

The example that made this obvious to me is a six-location fashion prompt that is really a single instruction about one lens. Prompts organised around each of the other rows in that table are at https://minimax-h3ai.video.
